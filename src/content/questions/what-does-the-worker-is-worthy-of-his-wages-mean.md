It means God's servants deserve to be provided for while they do His work. In context it teaches that they receive their living from those who welcome them and from the church, not that they may charge a fee for ministry.

## Proofs
- Luke 10:7
- Matthew 10:10
- 1 Timothy 5:18
- Deuteronomy 25:4

<!-- LONG_ANSWER -->

This saying is often used to justify charging for ministry. But look where Jesus first said it.

In Luke 10 He sends out the seventy-two with no money and tells them, "Stay at the same house, eating and drinking whatever you are offered. For the worker is worthy of his wages" (Luke 10:7). In Matthew 10 the same thought follows directly after "Freely you have received; freely give" and "Do not take along any gold or silver or copper in your belts" (Matthew 10:8-10).

So in the very passage where the saying appears, Jesus forbids charging. The "wages" are the food and lodging provided by those who receive His messengers. The worker deserves support; he does not set a price.

Paul quotes the same words in 1 Timothy 5:18, alongside "Do not muzzle an ox while it is treading out the grain" (Deuteronomy 25:4), to tell the *church* to honor its elders. Again the obligation falls on the church toward its servants. It is not permission for the servant to bill the people he serves.
