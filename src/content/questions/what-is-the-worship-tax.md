Most modern worship songs are copyrighted, so churches buy licenses to copy lyrics onto screens and paper, record and stream their services. In effect the church pays for permission to praise God in its own gathering.

## Proofs
- Ephesians 5:19
- Colossians 3:16
- Psalm 96:1-2
- John 2:16

<!-- LONG_ANSWER -->

God commands His people to "speak to one another with psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs" (Ephesians 5:19) and to teach and admonish one another as they sing (Colossians 3:16). Congregational singing is ministry of the Word, set to music.

Yet most churches pay annual license fees, often through a licensing company, for the right to:

- show song lyrics on a screen or print them in a bulletin;
- make recordings of services;
- livestream services that include copyrighted songs.

In the United States, the law already lets churches perform and display religious works during worship services at a place of worship. What licenses mostly pay for is making copies: slides, printed words, recordings and streams. (This is a general description, not legal advice.)

Whatever the legal details, the arrangement means that a church must pay rent on its own worship. Some have called it a "worship tax." Songwriters who write for the church deserve support, but that support should come as partnership, not as a toll every congregation must pay to sing.

Churches can sing the many hymns and psalms that are in the public domain, and they can encourage songwriters to dedicate new songs to the public domain so that the whole church can use them freely.
