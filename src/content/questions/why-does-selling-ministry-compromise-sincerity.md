When a minister profits from each act of ministry, his hearers cannot be sure whether he serves them for their good or for his gain. Paul refused payment so that no one could question his motives.

## Proofs
- 2 Corinthians 2:17
- 1 Thessalonians 2:3-5
- 2 Corinthians 12:14-15
- Acts 20:33-34

<!-- LONG_ANSWER -->

Paul contrasted himself with "so many others, who peddle the word of God for profit," and said instead, "in Christ we speak before God with sincerity" (2 Corinthians 2:17). Peddling and sincerity are set against each other.

He made the same point to the Thessalonians: "we never used words of flattery or any pretext for greed. God is our witness!" (1 Thessalonians 2:5). And to the Corinthians: "I am not seeking your possessions, but you... I will most gladly spend my money and myself" (2 Corinthians 12:14-15).

Think of a man selling sweet corn from a food truck. It is good corn, and selling it is honest work. But nobody imagines he is there out of the goodness of his heart; he wants the money. If a preacher sets up the same way, charging for each sermon or book, his hearers can no longer tell whether he cares about them, or even believes what he says, or simply wants their money.

A price introduces a second motive into the room. Is the counselor keeping the sessions going because the counselee needs them, or because they pay? Is the teacher softening a hard truth because it is wise, or because offended customers stop buying? Even when the minister's heart is pure, the arrangement invites the question. Paul gave up what he could have claimed precisely so that the question could never be asked of him.

Freely given ministry has nothing to gain from the hearer except the hearer's good.
